009                            ABSTRACT
010
011             Current interactive talking avatar models remain fundamentally unaware of dia-
012             logue context, requiring explicit textual or audio driving signals for each response,
013             thereby functioning more like video renderers than autonomous avatar agents.
014              Inspired by recent advances in unified understanding and generation frameworks,
            we present OmniPersona, an agentic model that integrates a pretrained multimodal015
                  large language model (MLLM) with an adapted joint audio-video generator. Our
016
              framework unifies dialogue understanding with synchronized audio-video avatar
017
                 generation within a single architecture. Central to this design are learnable queries
018               that compress historical multimodal context into a fixed-size non-verbal memory.
019             Together with verbal content, this memory enables our generator to produce more
020              responsive avatar behaviors, particularly in the listening state, which remains under-
021             explored in prior work centered on talking scenarios. Additionally, we incorporate
022             a suite of anti-drifting techniques, including a masked fusion attention design, to
023             support persona consistency (audio-visual identity and synchronization) across
024              multi-turn conversations. This attention design enables customized voice condi-
025              tioning without compromising audio-video consistency. Extensive experiments
              on three datasets and human evaluation demonstrate that OmniPersona achieves026
                 superior performance.

029    1  INTRODUCTION
030
031    The emergence of talking avatars has opened new possibilities for human-agent interaction, spanning
032     applications from virtual assistants to digital companions. Despite recent progress, current avatar
033     systems (Huang et al., 2025b; Gan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Low et al., 2025) remain unaware of
034     dialogue context: they treat conversation as a series of isolated lip-sync video generations, requiring
035      explicit textual descriptions or pre-generated audio for each response rather than a continuous, context-
036    aware dialogue. This limitation stems from an architectural disconnect: existing systems separate
         conversational understanding from visual generation, preventing avatars from naturally adapting their037
        behavior based on accumulated interaction history and non-verbal cues.
038
039     Recent breakthroughs in unified understanding and generation, exemplified by Omni-Video (Tan
040      et al., 2025) and UniVid (Luo et al., 2025b), demonstrate the promise of integrating multimodal
041     comprehension with visual generative capabilities within a unified architecture. However, none
042     of these approaches is designed for conversational interaction: which requires interactive (face-
         to-face dyadic communication), synchronized multimodal (text, audio, and video), and multi-turn043
         identity-preserving (audio-visual persona) dialogue generation.
044
045     In this direction, OmniResponse (Luo et al., 2025a) takes an initial step by integrating visual and
046     acoustic generation into an LLM pipeline via next-token prediction, enabling multimodal responses
047     spanning text, audio, and video. However, its reliance on low-dimensional, animation-based visual
048     representations and unsynchronized audio-video prediction substantially limits generation quality.
       SpeakerVid-5M (Zhang et al., 2026) adopts an MLLM as a backbone and injects textual embeddings as049
         conditions for joint audio-video generation; yet it focuses exclusively on speaking portrait generation,
050
        without modeling listener behavioral dynamics or supporting multi-turn dialogue.
051
052    To this end, we introduce OmniPersona, a unified model that seamlessly combines a pretrained
053     multimodal large language model (MLLM) with an adapted audio-video generative model to create
        identity-customizable avatars capable of context-aware, audio-synchronized, multi-turn interactions

075
       Modeling such behavioral dynamics requires recognizing that speaking and listening are not mutu-
076
         ally exclusive: a natural agent produces backchannels (e.g., “uh-huh”, “mm”) with corresponding
077      facial cues while listening, and exhibits natural pauses and rhythmic modulation while speaking.
078    OmniPersona addresses this via two parallel streams: a verbal stream that autoregressively generates
079      transcriptions from prior conversational context, and a non-verbal stream that distills affective cues
080      (e.g., tone, prosody, behavioral tendencies) into a compact memory condition via learnable queries.
081    Both streams jointly condition a downstream audio-video diffusion model, synchronizing what is
082     said and how it is naturally expressed.
083   A further challenge in auto-regressive diffusion generation is the drifting issue from teacher forc-
084     ing (Chen et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2026b; Xiao et al.,
085     2025; He et al., 2025). We address this through a suite of complementary anti-drifting techniques.
086      First, Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024) is adopted to mitigate temporal drifting by imposing
087     varying noise levels across sequential frames, reducing error propagation during auto-regressive
088      synthesis. Second, to prevent mutual interference between the audio and video streams, we introduce
089     a carefully designed Masked Fusion Cross Attention module within the cross-attention layers that
090     connect audio and video modalities, enabling customized voice conditioning without compromising
        audio-video consistency. Third, Strided RoPE augments rotary position embeddings with a strided091
        encoding scheme, sustaining long-range visual coherence. Together, these techniques enable stable,
092
         identity-preserving avatar generation over long conversational interactions.

      2  RELATED WORK
125
126
127     2.1  UNIFIED MODELS FOR UNDERSTANDING AND GENERATION
128
129     Recent advances have converged on architectures that unify visual understanding and generation
130     within single models. Show-o (Xie et al., 2025a), Show-o2 (Xie et al., 2025b) and BAGEL (Deng
131      et al., 2025) demonstrate that autoregressive and diffusion objectives can coexist in a single model,
132     scaling to both image and video modalities. BLIP3-o (Chen et al., 2025b) takes a complementary
133     route by generating semantically rich CLIP features via flow-matching diffusion transformers rather
134     than discrete token prediction. On the video side, UniVideo (Wei et al., 2026), Omni-Video (Tan et al.,
135     2025), and Omni-Video 2 (Yang et al., 2026a) establish that MLLMs can serve as semantic controllers
136     for diffusion-based video generation, while MetaQueries (Pan et al., 2025) offers a lightweight
        bridging mechanism that preserves a frozen MLLM’s understanding capabilities. Our work extends137
          this unified paradigm to multi-turn audio-video generation, where the model must condition on an
138
        evolving multimodal dialogue history.
139
140
141
        2.2  FROM TALKING TO AGENTIC AVATAR142
143
144     Audio-driven talking avatar methods (Yang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Gan et al., 2025; Zhu
145      et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026) take a complete audio sequence as input and generate the full
        video non-causally, making them unsuitable for interactive scenarios. Streaming systems (Guo et al.,146
        2025; Ki et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025b; Sun et al., 2026) also rely on complete audio input but
147
        generate video frames causally; since speech synthesis runs at low latency, these systems can be
148
       composed with an external MLLM to support low-latency interaction. In both cases, however, the
149      avatar module remains a passive renderer that depends entirely on upstream models for language and
150     speech decisions, with no access to dialogue history.
151
        This coupling bottleneck motivates agentic designs that integrate an MLLM into the generation152
         pipeline, enabling contextual understanding and generation within a unified framework. Body of
153
       Her (Ao, 2024) extends an LLM to jointly model speech and upper-body motion via next-token
154
         prediction. OmniResponse (Luo et al., 2025a) introduces temporal alignment tokens to synchronize
155      text and visual streams. SpeakerVid-5M (Zhang et al., 2026) pairs Qwen2.5-Omni (Xu et al.,
156     2025) with a hybrid autoregressive-diffusion head for person-generic generation. X-Streamer (Xie
157      et al., 2025c) separates conversational reasoning from video synthesis through a thinker-actor dual-
158     transformer, sustaining consistent interactions over long sessions. MIDAS (Chen et al., 2025c) routes
159     multimodal conditioning signals from a minimally modified LLM to a diffusion decoding head.
160     Following this paradigm, our method conditions generation on the accumulated dialogue history
161     to improve response accuracy while maintaining cross-turn audio-visual identity consistency and
        synchronization.

        3.2  CONNECTING MLLM WITH DIFFUSION MODEL
217
218   We build a connector that bridges the MLLM with the diffusion model by augmenting text condition-
219     ing with a non-verbal memory compressed from the multimodal dialogue context C (historical text,
220     video, and audio from both user and agent) via learnable queries.
221      Specifically, learnable queries Q ∈RNq×dm serve as the compression bottleneck, where Nq controls
222     the memory capacity and dm is the hidden dimension of Qwen2.5-Omni. We concatenate C and Q
223     and feed them into Qwen2.5-Omni thinker backbone to obtain contextualized query representations:
224
                                       (ˆC, ˆQ) = Qwen(C, Q),   ˆQ ∈RNq×dm.                           (4)225
226
227     Next, we discard ˆC and map ˆQ to the T5 hidden dimension dT5 with a zero-initialized projection layer
       and refine it with a K-layer Transformer encoder operating on the query sequence via self-attention:228
229               M n = TransEnc ProjT5(ˆQ)  ∈RNq×dT5.                          (5)
230
231    The resulting M n is concatenated with the T5 embeddings ET5 of the speech transcription T S to
232    form the augmented conditioning input for the diffusion generator:
233
                                                                           ˜e = [ET5; M n],                                        (6)
234
235    where [· ; ·] denotes sequence-dimension concatenation. This would be cross-attended by the noised
236      latent with the prefix and reference latent in the later stage for denoising purposes.

238     3.3  AUDIO-VIDEO JOINT GENERATION
239
     We build on OVI (Low et al., 2025), a pretrained DiT with a dual-branch architecture with bidirectional240
        cross-modal fusion for synchronized audio-video generation (Figure 3). Each modality branch has N
241
        transformer blocks with self-attention, text cross-attention, fusion cross-attention, and FFN.
242
243      Specifically, each branch concatenates its latents along the sequence dimension: [V P , V N, V R] for
244     video and [AP , AN, AR] for audio. Self-attention is then applied within each branch, where AN
245     attends to AR for timbre conditioning and AP for temporal continuity, while V N attends to V R for
         identity and V P for frame consistency. Next, the latents cross-attend to the verbal and non-verbal246
        condition embeddings ˜e, after which the two branches interact via fusion cross-attention.
247
248    The core design is asymmetric fusion cross-attention (Figure 3, right), where each branch cross-
249     attends to the other:
250                       Fusionvid = CrossAttn Q = [V P ; V N; V R], KV = [AP ; AN]  ,               (7)

274     3.4  MITIGATING VIDEO AND AUDIO DRIFTING
275
276     3.4.1  VIDEO ANTI-DRIFTING
277
       To reduce the training–inference gap caused by autoregressive prefix conditioning in the video, we278
        adopt Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024) and Strided RoPE in the video branch.
279
280     Diffusion Forcing.  Specifically, the noised video latent V N is partitioned into chunks of size c,
                      N     N     N                        P281     resulting in C chunks V  = [V1  , . . . , VC  ], together with the prefix V   , finally forms ˜V =
282     [V P , V1N , . . . , VCN ]. Note that the reference latent V R is kept clean throughout training to provide
283      stable identity guidance. Each chunk Vi in ˜V is assigned an independently sampled diffusion timestep:
284
                                      Vi = αtiVi + σtiϵi, ϵi ∼N(0, I).                              (9)285
286
        This chunk-wise noising prevents the model from relying on clean historical latents, encouraging it to
287                                                             R         extract only temporal continuity from the prefix while relying on the clean reference V   for identity
288     information, thereby reducing identity and color drift in multi-turn autoregressive generation.
289
290     Strided RoPE for Reference Anchoring. Since the reference image V R is discontinuous from ˜V , we
291      offset its RoPE positional index (Su et al., 2024). If the sequence length of ˜V is L, the reference
292     position is set to pR = L + δ, where δ ∼U(20, 50). This offset prevents V R from being interpreted
293     as a neighboring frame, allowing it to act as a global identity anchor that future frames converge to
        while preserving temporal consistency. Appendix D.3 explains the choice of the training offset range294
       and analyzes how different inference offsets affect identity consistency and pose variation across
295
        multiple turns.
296
297
        3.4.2  AUDIO ANTI-DRIFTING
298
299   We empirically observe that audio drifting is less severe than video drifting and can be effectively
300     mitigated with a simplified diffusion forcing strategy. During training, the noisy audio latent AN
301      is treated as a single chunk and noised with a diffusion timestep, while the prefix audio AP  is
302     independently noised and the reference audio AR remains clean. This training strategy improves
303     robustness against noisy prefix audio and stabilizes multi-turn autoregressive audio generation.
304     In addition, we isolate the reference audio AR from the visual stream in the fusion cross-attention
305     as Sec.  3.3. Introducing AR into audio-video fusion would couple speaker timbre with visual
306     generation and easily lead to audio-video misalignment. In practice, even short reference audio AR
307     in Fusionvid can cause abnormal visual endings, which then propagate back through Fusionaud
308     and corrupt the timbre representation. By restricting fusion to [AP , AN], while keeping AR only for
309     audio self-attention, we prevent this feedback loop and stabilize both audio and video generation.

        4.1  DATASETS AND EVALUATION
327
328     Training Dataset. We use Seamless Interaction dataset (Agrawal et al., 2025) for training, which is a
329     large-scale dyadic face-to-face interaction video dataset collected in laboratory setting. It contains
330    4,000+ hours in-person human face-to-face interaction videos with 4,000+ participants, featuring
331      naturalistic conversations between familiar pairs and professional actors. During training, we use all
332     samples from the training set and crop them to participants’ facial regions, resulting in videos with
333    448 × 448 resolution at 25 fps.
334     Evaluation Setup. We evaluate our method following the protocols of OmniResponse (Luo et al.,
335     2025a), SpeakerVid-5M (Zhang et al., 2026), and our introduced benchmark based on the Seam-
336     lessInteraction (Agrawal et al., 2025). OmniResponse assesses text reply accuracy and serves as a
337     multi-turn benchmark, yet lacks metrics for audio-video long-term consistency (e.g., identity preser-
338     vation and voice similarity) as well as evaluation of listening-phase behavior. SpeakerVid-5M offers
339    more comprehensive audio-video metrics, but is limited to short responder talking videos, making it
340      insufficient for capturing the full dynamics of multi-turn interaction.
341    To address these gaps, we introduce an additional evaluation on the SeamlessInteraction
342      dataset (Agrawal et al., 2025), comprising 60 randomly selected video clips from the test set spanning
343     three interaction states: mainly speaking, mainly listening, and rapid speaking–listening alternation,
344     each around 50 seconds long. We adopt five metrics: FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2018) to measure
345     long-term temporal video quality, ArcFace (Deng et al., 2019a) to evaluate identity consistency across
346     frames, SIM-o (Chen et al., 2025d) to assess voice similarity between generated and reference speech,
347    FIDemo (Deng et al., 2019b) and FD (Zhou et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025) to quantify the naturalness
       and accuracy of facial expressions during the listening phase.
348
349     Inference Efficiency. On four H200 GPUs, OmniPersona generates a 5-second, 448 × 448 clip
350     in 4.6 seconds using 10 diffusion steps, including MLLM encoding and VAE decoding. Timing
351     comparisons and configurations are detailed in Appendix E.
352

470    5  LIMITATION AND CONCLUSION
471
472     In this paper, we present OmniPersona, a unified framework that bridges pre-trained MLLMs with
473      joint audio–visual generation for interactive avatar agents. By compressing dialogue history into
474    compact non-verbal memory queries and combining them with the textual response, our system
475     enables coherent multi-turn conversations with natural listening and speaking behaviors. Together
476     with masked fusion attention and anti-drifting mechanisms, OmniPersona achieves stable iden-
          tity preservation, synchronized audio–visual generation, and temporally consistent long-horizon477
         interactions.
478
479     Despite these advantages, several limitations remain.  First, although our model achieves faster-
480     than-real-time clip generation on four H200 GPUs (Appendix E), limited computational resources
481     prevented us from training a distilled block-causal variant, which could further reduce latency and
482      better support streaming. Second, the current system focuses primarily on facial regions. We leave
        streaming-oriented interaction and full-body digital human generation for future work. Overall, we483
       hope OmniPersona can provide useful insights toward building more interactive and responsive digital
484
         agents.

       B.1  MODEL ARCHITECTURE
772
773    Our system combines a multimodal large language model (MLLM) with a diffusion-based joint
774     audio–video generation backbone. Specifically, we adopt Qwen2.5-Omni-7B as the MLLM backbone,
775    which provides high-level conversational reasoning. To bridge the MLLM and the diffusion generator,
776    we introduce a learnable connector that first maps the MLLM query representations to the T5 hidden
777     dimension through a zero-initialized projection layer and then refines them with Transformer encoder
778      layers. This connector transforms the MLLM outputs into conditioning tokens suitable for the
779     diffusion backbone.
780    The diffusion backbone is initialized from the pretrained OVI-v1.1 model, a 960 × 960 resolution
781     video diffusion model trained on 10-second clips. The generated video and audio latents are decoded
782     using Wan2.2 VAE and MMAudio VAE, respectively. In addition, a separate T5-XXL text encoder
783    (UMT5) provides textual conditioning signals for the diffusion process.

796    B.2  TRAINING SETUP
797
798     Training is conducted using bf16 mixed precision on 8× H200 GPUs for 48k steps, with a per-device
        batch size of 5. The model is optimized using AdamW with a cosine learning rate schedule, where799
        the learning rate decays to a minimum of 1 × 10−6 after a warmup of 100 steps. A weight decay
800
        of 0.1 is applied during optimization, and gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 is used to
801
          stabilize training. The loss weights for audio and video generation are set to λA = 0.5 and λV = 1.0,
802      respectively.
803
804    LoRA is applied to both the video and audio branches of the diffusion model, with the LoRA rank and
        alpha both set to 128. The LoRA parameters are trained with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5, while the805
     MLLM backbone remains frozen and only the connector module together with the token embedding
806
         layer are updated using a learning rate of 5 × 10−5.
807
808    To better model conversational dynamics, the training data is sampled with a 4:1 ratio between mainly
809     speaking segments and mainly listening segments. Furthermore, the loss for listening samples is
        doubled to encourage the model to learn richer non-verbal reactions.

822    B.3  CONDITIONING FOR THE DIFFUSION MODEL
823
824    The diffusion model is conditioned on seven inputs: the prefix video latent VP , the noised video latent
825    VN, a reference video latent VR, the prefix audio latent AP , the noised audio latent AN, a reference
826     audio latent AR, and a response embedding ˜e.
827
       Video latents are produced by a 3D Causal VAE. The first latent frame corresponds to 1 video frame,
828
        while each subsequent latent frame corresponds to 4 frames. For reference image VR, it is randomly
829
         selected from the whole video clip during training while provided by user during inference. During
830      training, we either select the prefix latent length as 1 or 5 (1 or 17 frames, also 0.04 s or 0.68 s at
831    25 FPS), so that the model can handle both scenarios at inference time: the first turn where only a
832     single reference image is available, and later turns where a longer prefix segment can be extracted
833     from already generated video to maintain temporal continuity. The target video latent VN is set as 31
834      latent frames (about 4.96 s) for training.
835
       Audio and video latents are temporally aligned according to the downsampling of video and audio
836    VAEs. Given a video frame rate of 25 FPS, audio sampling rate of 16 kHz, and audio VAE downsam-
837                                                        25/4
        pling factor of 512, the temporal ratio becomes 16000/512 = 0.2, meaning that 1 video latent frame838
        corresponds to 5 audio latent frames. Since the first latent frame of the 3D Causal VAE represents
839
        only one frame rather than 4, we append 3 silent audio latent frames in addition to the true aligned
840     audio to maintain the fixed audio–video temporal ratio.
841
842     For prefix audio latent AP and target audio latent AN at training, they are extracted from the whole
        audio clip to temporally align with VP , VN respectively. As for the reference audio, a 4-6 s speaking843
        audio clip is randomly selected from the whole audio during training while provided by the user
844
        during inference.
845
846    The response embedding ˜e concatenates non-verbal memory tokens and a T5-based verbal response
847     representation. The memory tokens are produced by the MLLM encoding the audio–video context
848     of the previous conversational turn from both the user and the agent (5–20s during training and
         inference), while the verbal response is generated by the MLLM conditioned on the full dialogue849
         history.
850
851     During training, classifier-free guidance is applied with a dropout ratio of 0.1 to both the response
852    embedding ˜e and the reference audio latent AR.

    C  HUMAN EVALUATION PROTOCOL
872
873
     We invited 20 participants to evaluate 20 dialogue pairs randomly sampled from the 60 pairs in our874
        SeamlessInteraction evaluation, comparing OmniPersona, LiveAvatar, and OmniAvatar. Participants
875
       viewed the full dialogue, with speaking and listening intervals explicitly marked, and rated each
876
         criterion on a 1–5 Mean Opinion Score (MOS) scale.
877

878
       General instructions and evaluation scope.  Before rating, participants were instructed to judge
879
        each criterion independently, avoid carrying impressions from one criterion into another, and disregard
880
        personal preferences for the person’s appearance. Listening expression appropriateness (EA) assessed
881     contextual appropriateness within the marked listening intervals. Audio-visual synchronization
882    (AVS) assessed synchronization during speaking intervals. Overall quality (OQ) assessed audio-
883     visual artifacts and identity consistency over the full video. This separation was intended to reduce
884     interference between criteria; the three scores should be interpreted jointly, since high EA does not
885     compensate for poor synchronization or identity consistency.
886    The AVS and OQ criteria are similar to those commonly used in prior talking-avatar evaluations,
887     so we do not elaborate on their scoring procedures here. We focus instead on EA, which evaluates
888      listening behavior in its conversational context and requires explicit guidance on how to judge the
889     appropriateness of a listener’s reactions. We therefore detail the EA instructions and rating anchors
890     below.
891
892     Listening expression appropriateness. EA was a composite contextual-appropriateness rating,
893      rather than separate subscores for emotion, timing, and intensity. It considered semantic and emotional
894     congruence with the conversation, reaction timing, and reaction intensity and attentiveness. Image
895      quality, identity consistency, and speaking lip synchronization were assessed through the other
896      criteria rather than the EA score. After the general instructions, participants received the following
897     EA-specific prompt:
898
                 Please focus only on the person while they are listening. Considering what the other
899
                person is saying, their tone of voice, and the surrounding dialogue context, how
900
                 appropriate is the listener’s facial reaction at that moment? Consider whether the re-
901
                  action matches the meaning and emotion of the conversation and whether its timing
902            and intensity are appropriate rather than emotionally unrelated or unresponsive.
903
904     This prompt was specific to EA and was not the complete instruction set. Before the formal evaluation,
905      participants viewed examples corresponding to different rating levels to calibrate their interpretation
906     of the criterion and scale.
907
                        Table 9: Rating anchors for listening expression appropriateness.
908
909          Score   Criterion
910          1     Completely inappropriate: the expression clearly conflicts with the context, such as an
911                   opposite emotion or an evidently incorrect reaction time.
912          2     Mostly inappropriate: the expression is largely inconsistent in emotion, timing, or intensity.
913          3     Acceptable: the expression is generally plausible but may be neutral, ambiguous, weakly
                       responsive, or occasionally mismatched.
914
             4     Appropriate: the expression aligns well with the conversational meaning, emotion, and timing,

       Broader impact.  OmniPersona aims to make multimodal human-agent interaction more natural and1053
