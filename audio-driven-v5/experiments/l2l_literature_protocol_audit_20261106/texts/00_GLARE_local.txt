                                   Abstract

 1         While talking head generation has advanced rapidly, generating natural listener
 2          behavior in dyadic conversations, which know when to react, how to react, and
 3          with what type of response, remains underexplored.  Existing dyadic datasets
 4           lack fine-grained listener reaction annotations, and prevailing evaluation metrics
 5            inherited from talking-head and video generation measure visual realism rather than
 6          whether a listener reacted appropriately. We address these gaps along three aspects.
 7             First, we curate a listening-head-specific dataset built from RealTalk and Seamless
 8            Interaction, comprising approximately 147 hours of paired speaker–listener videos
 9          with 64,557 event-level reaction annotations across six categories: nodding, head
10           shaking, smiling, laughing, frowning, and surprised. Second, we introduce an
11           audio-driven baseline built on a flow-matching transformer, namely GLARE, with
12          prosody conditioning derived from Qwen2-Audio and a temporal reaction loss that
13            explicitly supervises frame-wise reactions. Third, we propose a reaction-oriented
14           evaluation protocol that jointly measures reaction occurrence (R-F1), temporal
15          alignment (R-tIoU), asymmetric temporal deviation (R-ATD), and reaction-region
16           visual quality (R-FID), giving a more behaviorally grounded assessment than
17           visual-quality-only metrics. Experiment results show consistent gains over prior
18            listening-head methods in both visual fidelity and reaction-level metrics, suggesting
19            that reaction-aware data, modeling, and evaluation are critical for natural listening
20           behavior.

21  1  Introduction

22  Recent advances in talking head generation have greatly improved lip synchronization, identity
23  preservation, and photorealistic rendering [14; 22; 32; 16; 18; 44; 12; 48]. Most existing methods,
24  however, focus on animating the speaker. In face-to-face conversation, the listener is equally important:
25  non-verbal behaviors, such as brief reactions like smiling and nodding, convey attention, agreement,
26   hesitation, and affective engagement [41]. The timing and type of these reactions strongly affect
27  perceived naturalness, since visually plausible motions can still appear unnatural when reactions are
28  absent, mistimed, or inconsistent with the conversational context [24; 25; 9].

29  Listening head generation has recently emerged as a distinct task. ViCo [47] introduced an early
30  benchmark, while subsequent works explored non-deterministic listener motion, language-aware
31  responses, emotion conditioning, improved sequence modeling, and real-time dyadic interaction [25;
32  26; 35; 20; 48; 15]. Despite this progress, reaction-aware listening head generation remains limited
33  by two key bottlenecks. First, existing datasets are not designed around fine-grained listener reactions.
34  ViCo provides task-specific supervision but is limited in scale, while larger dyadic resources such as
35  RealTalk [12], Seamless Interaction [1], and SpeakerVid-5M [45] offer rich conversational videos
36  without event-level annotations of reaction type, timing, and duration. As a result, current data
37  provide insufficient supervision for modeling when a listener should react, what reaction should
38  occur, and how the reaction should unfold over time.

39  Second, existing evaluation protocols mainly rely on metrics inherited from talking-head or video
40  generation, such as reconstruction fidelity, perceptual realism, and motion quality. These metrics are
41   useful for measuring global visual quality, but they do not explicitly assess whether generated listener
42   reactions are type-consistent, temporally aligned with conversational cues, or visually realistic within
43  reaction segments. This is particularly important because listener feedback is inherently multi-valid:
44  an appropriate response may not exactly match a single reference timestamp, yet its type, timing, and
45   quality should still be evaluated in a reaction-aware manner [9].

46  To address these limitations, we revisit listening head generation from the perspectives of data,
47  modeling, and evaluation. We curate a listening-head-specific dataset from RealTalk and Seamless
48   Interaction by extracting aligned speaker-listener portrait pairs and annotating listener reactions as
49  categorized temporal events. The resulting dataset contains approximately 147 hours of paired video
50  data and 64,557 reaction instances. Based on this dataset, we introduce an audio-driven listening
51  head generation method GLARE with prosody conditioning and a temporal reaction loss, enabling
52  the model to better capture speaker-side cues and generate appropriate listener reactions at the right
53  moment. We further propose reaction-centric evaluation metrics that measure reaction occurrence,
54  temporal alignment, asymmetric timing deviation, and reaction-region visual quality.

55  Our main contributions are as follows:

56   1. We curate a listening-head dataset built from dyadic conversational videos, with aligned video-
57      audio pairs and event-level annotations of listener reactions, comprising approximately 147 hours
58      of video data and 64,557 reaction instances.

59   2. We introduce an audio-driven reacting listener, namely GLARE, for listening head generation,
60     equipped with a dedicated prosody conditioning mechanism and a temporal reaction loss.

61   3. We propose reaction-oriented evaluation metrics for listening head generation that jointly measures
62      visual fidelity, reaction accuracy, and temporal alignment, enabling finer-grained assessment of
63      conversational appropriateness with extensive experiments comparing existing methods.


64  2  Related Works


65  Listening head generation.  Listening head generation synthesizes non-verbal listener behaviors
66  conditioned on speaker-side conversational cues. ViCo [46] first established responsive listening head
67  generation as a benchmark, and Learning2Listen [25] modeled listener motion as a non-deterministic
68   distribution to reflect the multi-valid nature of dyadic interaction. Later methods improved listener
69  synthesis with emotion conditioning [35], non-autoregressive sequence modeling [20], diffusion-
70  based generation [38], dyadic interaction modeling [42], and real-time multimodal interaction [48; 15].
71  However, most existing methods optimize holistic motion realism, diversity, or speaker-listener
72  synchrony, without explicitly modeling listener reactions as categorized and temporally localized
73   events. Our work instead focuses on generating reactions with appropriate type, timing and duration.


74  Interactive conversation datasets.  Dyadic conversation datasets provide important resources for
75  modeling social interaction. Early corpora such as RECOLA [33] and NoXi [5] support affective and
76  feedback behavior analysis, while ViCo [46] introduced a task-specific benchmark for responsive
77   listening heads. Larger resources such as RealTalk [12], Seamless [1], and SpeakerVid-5M [45]
78  provide more diverse conversational videos, but they are not designed around fine-grained listener
79  reaction events. In contrast, our dataset constructs aligned speaker-listener clips with explicit event-
80   level annotations of reaction type, timing, and duration.


81  Evaluation methods.  Listener generation is commonly evaluated using backchannel prediction
82  metrics, such as precision, recall, F1, tolerance windows, and subjective appropriateness judg-
83  ments [23; 10], or visual generation metrics such as FID [34], FVD [39], and LPIPS [43]. These
84  metrics measure feedback timing or global visual quality, but do not jointly assess whether a gener-
85  ated listener reacts with the correct type, at the correct moment, and with realistic reaction-specific
86  motion. Recent reaction generation benchmarks emphasize appropriateness, diversity, synchrony, and
87  realism [36; 37]. Our evaluation further treats reactions as categorized temporal events and measures
88  reaction occurrence, temporal alignment, asymmetric timing deviation, and reaction-region quality.

             89  3  Dataset Curation

             90  Our goal is to construct a listening-head-specific dataset that supports not only audio-driven listener
             91  head generation, but also fine-grained modeling and evaluation of listener reactions. To this end,
             92  we curate dyadic conversational videos into temporally aligned speaker–listener pairs and further
             93  annotate the listener’s visible reactions as categorized temporal events. The overall pipeline is shown
             94   in Fig. 1. Starting from RealTalk [11] and Seamless Interaction [1], we obtain 107,149 speaker
             95   audio/listener-video pairs, corresponding to approximately 147 hours of data.


             96  3.1  Speaker–Listener Clip Construction

             97  As shown in Fig.1 (2) and (3), we first process the raw dyadic videos to obtain high-quality portrait
             98   clips for both conversational participants. Since the original videos may contain scene transitions,
             99  black frames, irrelevant content, multiple visible people, severe occlusions, or unstable face crops,
            100  visual quality filtering is performed before clip extraction. For retained videos, each participant is
            101  cropped into a portrait-centered video and an additional manual face-quality check is conducted to
            102  remove clips with missing, partial, or poorly framed faces. This step ensures that the resulting listener
            103  videos contain stable facial regions suitable for motion generation and reaction annotation.

            104 We then parse the speaking and listening roles over time. For RealTalk, where the two speakers may
            105  be mixed in the original audio, we perform audio separation, denoising, and speaker diarization to
            106  estimate participant-level speech activity. For Seamless Interaction, we use the provided high-quality
            107  audio streams and diarization results, with timestamp correction when necessary. Based on the
            108   resulting speech-activity timelines, each dyadic conversation is decomposed into speaker-dominant
            109   intervals. For each interval, we pair the active speaker’s audio with the temporally synchronized
            110   portrait video of the other participant, who is treated as the listener. This produces paired training
            111  examples in the form of speaker audio and listener video, while preserving the conversational timing
            112  between the two participants.

            113  Implementation details of speaker-listener clip reconstruction, including filtering criteria, face-crop
            114  procedure, diarization correction, and quality-control procedures, are provided in Appendix A.

            115  3.2  Reaction Taxonomy and Annotation

            116 A central objective of our dataset is to explicitly capture listener reactions that are semantically
            117  meaningful and temporally localized. We focus on six common reaction categories: nodding, head
            118  shaking, smiling, laughing, frowning, and surprised, which are visually recognizable and frequently
            119  associated with conversational feedback and affective responses [25; 47; 12]. Compared with micro-
            120  behaviors (e.g., eye blinking or gaze shifts), and background motions (e.g., subtle pose adjustments),
            121  these reactions have four desirable properties: they convey clear communicative or affective intent,
            122   are visually recognizable and are strongly correlated with the conversational context. These properties
            123  make them suitable targets for reaction-aware listener generation and reaction-centric evaluation.

            124  As shown in Fig.1 (4), to annotate listener reactions at scale, we design a visual reaction detector to
            125  the cropped listener videos. For a listener clip with T frames, the detector outputs a dense reaction
            126  score vector r ∈[0, 1]T ×6, where each channel corresponds to one of the six reaction categories.
            127  The detector combines head-motion cues, facial landmark dynamics, facial expression cues, and
            128  temporal smoothing to estimate frame-wise reaction confidence. Continuous high-confidence regions
            129   are then converted into event-level annotations A = {(yi, ri, ts,i, te,i)}Ni=1, where yi ∈Y is the event
            130  type from the six reaction classes, ri is the frame-wise reaction score within [ts,i, te,i], and ts,i and
            131   te,i are the start and end times. Each final event is assigned a single type, and cross-class overlaps
            132  are resolved by keeping the dominant reaction. The resulting annotations therefore provide both
            133  sparse temporal boundaries for event-level evaluation and dense frame-wise scores for reaction-aware
            134   training supervision.

            135 We further conduct manual verification to remove unreliable detections and improve annotation
            136   quality. The final curated dataset contains 64,557 reaction instances in total. The distribution across
            137   reaction categories is summarized in Table 1. Smiling and head shaking are the most frequent reaction
            138   types, while surprised reactions occur less often, reflecting their relatively sparse and context-specific
            139  nature in natural conversations. The detailed detector design, including class-specific visual cues,
            140  smoothing strategies, event merging rules, and thresholds, can be referred to Appendix B.

            200  the visual quality of these reactions is satisfactory. To address these limitations, we propose a set of
            201  reaction-centric evaluation metrics that explicitly measure the reaction type correctness, temporal
            202  alignment, and visual quality of generated listener reactions.

            228 We use asymmetric weights with α > β to place stronger penalties on negative deviations, which
            229  correspond to premature or truncated reactions, while allowing greater tolerance for slightly delayed
            230  or temporally extended reactions. Lower R-ATD indicates better temporal alignment and fewer
            231  premature or duration-inaccurate reactions.

            232  Reaction Fréchet distance (R-FID). R-FID evaluates the visual quality of generated reaction regions.
            233  Instead of computing FID over all video frames, which can be dominated by neutral listening frames,
            234  we compute the standard Fréchet distance only on frames inside reaction intervals. Generated reaction
            235  frames are collected from predicted reaction segments, while real reaction frames are collected from
            236  ground-truth reaction annotations. Lower R-FID indicates that the generated reaction frames better
            237  match the visual distribution of real listener reactions.

            238  Together, R-F1, R-tIoU, R-ATD, and R-FID provide complementary measurements of reaction cor-
            239   rectness, temporal localization, asymmetric timing deviation, and reaction-region realism. Although
            240  multiple reactions may be plausible for the same context, our evaluation only compares against
            241  the ground truth, serving as a reference rather than an absolute measure of reaction plausibility.
            242  Additional implementation details, including one-to-one matching, aggregation, empty-case handling,
            243  R-ATD penalization with α and β, and R-FID computation, are provided in Appendix D.

            244  6  Experiments

            245  6.1  Experiment Settings

            246  Implementation details. We implement our listening-head generator with a latent flow-matching
            247  transformer and train it with mixed precision using Accelerate on 4×L40s GPUs. We use AdamW
            248   (lr=5e-4), gradient clipping (1.0), cosine decay with warmup, batch size 256, and train for 650 epochs.
            249  The default temporal setup is 25 FPS, and audio is sampled at 16 kHz. Input frames are resized to
            250  512×512 and normalized to [−1, 1]. We split the train/test set into a ratio 9:1. The pretrained motion
            251  autoencoder [40] and audio LLM [8] are kept frozen. More details can be found in Appendix C.1.

            252  Evaluation metrics. We use Fréchet Inecption Distance (FID) [34] and 16-frame Fréchet Video
            253  Distance (FVD) [39] to assess image and video generation quality; Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio
            254  (PSNR) for measuring pixel fidelity; Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) for perceived change in
            255   structural information; Variation (Var) [25] for motion diversity and richness; Learned Perceptual
            256  Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [43] for measuring perceptual distance; Residual Pearson Correlation
            257   Coefficient (rPCC) to measure the correlation between motions of speaker and listener; DI-Sync [27]
            258   to quantify the causal coordination between the speaker’s verbal cues and the listener’s non-verbal
            259  responses. We also use metrics proposed in Sec. 5 (i.e., R-F1, R-tIoU, R-ATD, and R-FID) to

            287  6.3  Ablation Study

            288 We conduct our ablation studies on RealTalk dataset. More ablation studies, including speaker audio
            289   length, prosody channel projection, and reaction loss type, are included in appendix E.2

            290  Module effectiveness. We ablate the effectiveness of the prosody conditioning mechanism and
            291  temporal reaction loss in Table 4a.  It can be seen that, adding the prosody condition improves
            292  visual quality and motion dynamics (e.g., PSNR, FVD, Var), but brings limited gains on reaction-
            293   related metrics and even degrades temporal precision (R-ATD), indicating that prosodic cues alone
            294  are insufficient for accurate reaction modeling.  In contrast, the reaction loss yields substantial
            295  improvements across various metrics (DI-Sync, R-F1, R-tIoU) and significantly reduces R-ATD and
            296  R-FID, demonstrating its key role in learning temporally aligned and semantically correct reactions.
            297  Combining both modules achieves the best overall performance, showing that prosody provides
            298  complementary temporal cues on top of explicit reaction supervision.

            299  Reaction loss coefficient λreact. Table 4b shows that increasing the reaction loss coefficient λreact
            300  from a small value (0.01) to a moderate range (0.05–0.1) consistently improves both visual quality
            301  and reaction-related metrics, indicating that stronger supervision helps the model learn more accurate
            302  and temporally aligned behaviors. However, further increasing λreact (e.g., ≥0.2) leads to degraded
            303  performance in both generation quality and reaction accuracy, suggesting that overly strong supervi-
            304  sion disrupts the balance between the two objectives. Overall, λreact=0.05 achieves the best trade-off
            305  across metrics.

            462  Seamless Interaction offers a substantially larger collection of dyadic audiovisual recordings with
            463  high-resolution videos and cleaner audios. Since neither dataset is originally organized around
            464  listening-head generation with temporally localized listener reaction annotations, we further process
            465  the raw videos into aligned speaker-audio/listener-video pairs and annotate listener reactions at the
            466  event level.

            467  A.2  Video Filtering and Face-Crop Quality Control

            468  The raw videos contain several types of content that are unsuitable for listening head generation,
            469  including abrupt scene transitions, blackout frames, irrelevant scenes, severe occlusion, unstable
            470  viewpoints, and cases where more than two people appear in the visible region. To remove these
            471  samples efficiently, we uniformly sample frames from each video at 2.5-second intervals and perform
            472  a thumbnail-based inspection. Videos with frequent scene changes, missing faces, severe visual
            473   artifacts, or excessive participant overlap are discarded. We then conduct a second verification pass
            474  on the retained videos to further remove visually unstable or semantically irrelevant clips.

            475  For each retained dyadic video, we separately crop the two participants into portrait-centered videos.
            476 We use Face-Alignment [4] to detect the facial region in the first frame where a valid face is observed,
            477  enlarge the detected bounding box by a scale factor of 1.5, and apply the resulting crop to the
            478   full video. The enlargement preserves contextual head motion and avoids overly tight crops that
            479  would remove meaningful listener reactions. After cropping, we re-run face detection and landmark
            480   localization on the cropped videos to identify low-quality samples. A cropped video is discarded if it
            481  contains missing face detections, partial faces, abnormal facial aspect ratios, unstable face proportions,
            482  or strong non-frontal views that prevent reliable motion extraction and reaction annotation.


            483  A.3  Audio Processing and Speaker–Listener Pairing

            484  For RealTalk, the raw audio contain mixed speech from both participants. We first apply the Asteroid
            485   toolkit [28] for source separation and then perform audio denoising to improve the quality of each
            486  separated stream. The separated streams are manually checked to remove severe separation failures.
            487 We then use PyAnnote [31; 3] to estimate speech-activity boundaries for each participant. These
            488  boundaries define the time intervals in which one participant is treated as the active speaker and the
            489  other participant is treated as the listener.

            490  For Seamless Interaction, the audio streams are generally cleaner and often already separated, with
            491  small portion of them including noises. We use the provided audio and diarization information
            492  when reliable, and apply PyAnnote-based timestamp correction when the provided boundaries are
            493  inaccurate. After obtaining participant-level speech-activity timelines, we decompose each dyadic
            494  conversation into a sequence of speaker-dominant intervals. For each interval, we pair the active
            495  speaker’s audio with the temporally synchronized cropped video of the other participant (listener).
            496  This produces paired samples of the form (aspeaker, V listener), where aspeaker denotes the speaker audio
                                listener            497  and V      denotes the listener video.

            515 B  Reaction Detector Details


            516  B.1  Frame-Wise Reaction Representation

            517  For each cropped listener video with T frames, the reaction detector outputs a dense frame-wise
            518  score map r ∈[0, 1]T ×K, where K = 6 corresponds to nodding, head shaking, smiling, laughing,
            519  frowning, and surprised. The k-th channel r(k)t   represents the confidence that reaction type k occurs
            520   at frame t. We convert the dense scores into event-level annotations by thresholding each channel,
            521   extracting connected temporal components, removing isolated short detections, and merging adjacent
            522  components separated by short gaps. Each retained component is stored as an event (yi, ri, ts,i, te,i),
            523  where yi is the reaction class, ri is the score vector, and si, ei are the start and end frames.

            524  Formally, for reaction class k, we define that the reaction score for a frame at time t is 0 if the reaction
            525  detection confidence is lower than the corresponding threshold θk:

                                                       r(k)t = 0 h r(k)t  ≤θk i ,                                     (8)

            526  where θk = 0.5 is a fixed threshold for all six classes. The dense reaction scores are used as
            527  supervision for the temporal reaction loss, while the event-level annotations are used for reaction-
            528   centric evaluation.

            529   It is noteworthy that event-level annotations are single-label.  If candidate events from different
            530  reaction classes overlap in time, we keep the event with the highest averaged confidence score over
            531  the overlapping region, while discarding or trimming lower-confidence candidates. Ambiguous
            532  overlaps without a clear dominant visual cue are removed during manual verification to ensure a
            533   single event type yi.


            534  B.2  Head-Motion-Based Reactions

            535 We detect nodding and head shaking mainly from landmark-based head-motion trajectories [21]. A
            536  Face Alignment Network predicts facial landmarks for each valid frame. For nodding, we compute
            537  a vertical head-center coordinate as a weighted combination of stable facial landmarks, including
            538  the nose tip, chin, and outer eyebrow corners. We normalize this coordinate by a face-scale estimate
            539  derived from jaw width and the nose–chin distance, which reduces sensitivity to crop size and
            540   identity-specific face shape. Missing detections are forward-filled from the most recent valid estimate,
            541  and remaining short gaps are linearly interpolated.

            562  B.3  Expression-Based Reactions

            563  Smiling, laughing, frowning, and surprised reactions are detected from facial expression cues. We
            564  combine landmark dynamics, facial action-unit responses, and emotion predictions from facial
            565  expression analysis tools [7; 13]. The detector produces per-frame class confidence scores and then
            566  applies temporal smoothing and connected-component extraction to obtain event-level annotations.
            567  Table 6 summarizes the main visual cues used for each reaction class.

            568  The expression-based scores are temporally smoothed before thresholding.  This reduces false
            569   positives caused by single-frame expression estimation noise while preserving reaction onsets and
            570   offsets. Although multiple reaction cues may be activated within the same temporal region, the
            571   final event-level annotations are single-label and temporally non-overlapping across reaction classes.
            572 We first extract class-specific candidate intervals and then resolve cross-class overlaps by retaining
            573  the candidate with the highest averaged confidence score and the clearest visual evidence. Lower-
            574  confidence overlapping candidates are suppressed, and ambiguous cases are removed during manual
            575   verification. This design avoids assigning multiple event types to the same listener behavior.

            576  B.4  Manual Verification

            577  After automatic detection, we manually inspect the detected reaction events to improve annotation
            578   reliability. During verification, we remove events caused by tracking failure, scene change, face
            579  occlusion, unstable crops, non-listening behavior, or expression ambiguity. We also discard events
            580  whose visual evidence is too weak to support the assigned reaction label. This verification step is
            581  applied after the detector has produced candidate intervals, so the final annotations retain the temporal
            582  consistency of the automatic detector while reducing obvious false positives.

            702  E.2  More Ablation Studies

            703  Due to space limit in the main paper, here we present three more ablation studies, including input
            704  speaker audio length, prosody channel projection, and temporal reaction loss type.

            705  Input speaker audio length. Different length of input audio from speaker can provide different
            706  amount of contexts.  Table 9 shows that increasing speaker’s audio length generally improves
            707  performance, as longer temporal context provides richer cues for both motion generation and reaction
            708   prediction. Performance improves notably from 2s to 10s across most metrics, including visual quality
            709  (PSNR, FVD) and reaction accuracy (DI-Sync, R-F1, R-tIoU). However, further increasing the length
            710   (e.g., 20s or 50s) brings marginal gains or even slight degradation, likely due to increased temporal
            711  redundancy and modeling difficulty. Overall, 10s achieves the best balance between sufficient context
            712  and stable modeling.

            713  Prosody Channel Projection. In our architecture, we project the channel dimension from 1 of frame-
            714  aligned prosodic intensity scalar p into 4 of prosody condition w˜p. We ablate this channel projection
            715   in Table 10. The results show that introducing a learnable channel projection for the prosodic
            716   intensity signal consistently improves performance compared to using the raw scalar (channel=1),
            717   indicating the benefit of increasing representation capacity for prosody conditioning. As the channel
            718  dimension increases from 1 to 4, we observe steady gains in both generation quality (e.g., lower FVD,
            719  LPIPS, and rPCC) and reaction-related metrics (e.g., higher R-tIoU and lower R-ATD), suggesting
            720  improved temporal alignment and more accurate reaction dynamics. However, further increasing the
            721  channel dimension to 8 does not bring consistent improvements and slightly degrades several metrics,
            722  implying diminishing returns and potential over-parameterization. Overall, projecting the prosodic
            723   signal to a moderate channel size (4) achieves the best trade-off between expressiveness and stability,
            724  and is thus adopted in our final model.

            732 F  Discussion of Limitations and Future Works

            733  Despite the improvements achieved in reaction-aware listening head generation, several limitations
            734  remain. First, listener reactions are inherently multi-valid and subjective: given the same conversa-
            735   tional cue, different listeners may exhibit different reaction types, timings, or intensities. Our current
            736  formulation evaluates generated reactions against a single reference, which cannot fully capture this
            737   diversity. Second, our work focuses on a set of explicit actual reactions (e.g., nodding, smiling),
            738  while listener behavior, more broadly, also includes micro-behaviors (e.g., subtle gaze or facial
            739  changes) and baseline dynamics (e.g., natural head motion and posture drift showing the person is
            740  not stationary). These components are more continuous and harder to annotate, and are not explicitly
            741  modeled in our current framework.

            742  In addition, listener behavior is influenced by factors beyond audio prosody, such as language
            743  semantics, personality, and conversational context, which are only partially captured in our current
            744  model. Extending toward richer multimodal understanding and more comprehensive modeling of
            745   listener motion remains an important direction for future work.

            746 G  Broader Impact

            747  This work aims to improve the naturalness and appropriateness of listener behavior in conversational
            748  agents, with potential applications in virtual assistants, social robots, telepresence, and human-
            749  computer interaction systems. By enabling more responsive and context-aware listener reactions, our
            750  approach may contribute to more engaging and human-like interactions.

            751  At the same time, improved generation of realistic listener behaviors may introduce risks related to
            752   synthetic media, such as creating misleading or deceptive conversational content. In addition, listener
            753  reactions can implicitly convey agreement or emotional alignment, which may be misinterpreted
            754  or misused in certain contexts. We emphasize that this work is intended for research purposes,
            755  and responsible deployment should include appropriate safeguards such as clear disclosure of syn-
            756   thetic content, adherence to dataset licensing terms, and consideration of fairness and diversity in
            757  conversational behaviors.
