1. Introduction
005
Natural conversation requires both speaking and lis-
006
tening. While one participant holds the conversational
007
floor, the listener conveys attention and engagement
008
through facial expressions, head movements, and brief
009
vocal acknowledgments [13, 17]. These backchannels
010
support the ongoing utterance without taking over the
011
conversation. Visual reactions may occur with or with-
012
out vocal feedback, and quiet listening is also appropri-
013
ate. Digital humans therefore need to produce appro-
014
priate listening behavior as well as fluent spoken re-
015
sponses: realistic faces and voices alone do not estab-
016
lish whether feedback fits the conversational context.
017
Speech-driven
talking-head
methods
such
as
018
OmniHuman-1.5
[16]
synthesize
visual
behavior
019
from the target person’s supplied speech. Listening-
020
generation methods, including ViCo [27], Learning to
021
Listen [13], and GLARE [11], instead generate visual
022
reactions to another speaker without synthesizing
023
the listener’s audio.
Interactive methods such as
024
INFP [12] and DyStream [8] model speaking and
025
listening from both participants’ audio tracks, with
026
the target person’s vocal behavior supplied as an
027
input.
OmniResponse [18] and OmniPersona [17]
028
further generate audiovisual conversational responses,
029
including listener feedback.
Building on these ad-
030
vances, we focus on listening feedback as the primary
031
target of learning and evaluation, and investigate how
032
listening-specific conditioning, supervision, and data
033
can support its visual and vocal expression.
034
We study audiovisual backchannel generation from
035
the current speaker audio and a reference image of the
036
listener (Fig. 1). This setting supports interaction with
037
a visible digital avatar without requiring the user to
038
enable a camera, and accommodates initial or stan-
039
dalone exchanges with no prior dialogue history. The
040
listener’s audio is generated rather than supplied: the
041
model must produce visual reactions, brief vocal feed-
042
back, and quiet listening while the speaker retains the
043
floor.
044
Learning this behavior presents two related chal-
045
lenges.
First, the input speech belongs to the other
046
participant: it provides semantic and acoustic cues,
047
but does not prescribe the listener’s articulation or re-
048
action. These cues must be mapped to conditions suit-
049
able for generating the listener’s audio and video. Sec-
050
ond, feedback can be brief, subtle, and interspersed
051
with quiet listening. A media-generation objective su-
052
pervises the audiovisual output as a whole, but does
053
not explicitly specify which feedback categories should
054
occur.
This motivates event supervision that makes
055
feedback behavior a direct target of learning.
056
Our framework addresses these challenges through
057
direct speaker-audio conditioning and supervision of
058
both conditioning representations and feedback events.
059
Speaker-audio cross-attention supplies acoustic infor-
060
mation to the generator, while target audiovisual fea-
061
ture supervision trains the conditioning mapping to
062
retain information relevant to the listener’s response.
063
Clip-level event supervision complements the media-
064
generation objective by encouraging the corresponding
065
feedback categories to occur. This auxiliary objective
066
supervises category presence over the clip without im-
067
posing exact event timestamps.
068
To support listening-focused learning,
we con-
069
struct paired audiovisual data from Seamless conver-
070
sations [25], updating speaker–listener roles as turns
071
change while retaining brief vocal backchannels as lis-
072
tening feedback.
We use extracted visual and vocal
073
cues to guide Qwen3-Omni [21] in producing ordered
074
reaction descriptions, linking speaker audio with lis-
075
tener audiovisual media and annotations of their feed-
076
back.
077
We formulate a listening-focused evaluation design
078
that considers audiovisual quality, feedback-category
079
agreement, contextual appropriateness and engage-
080
ment, and audiovisual coordination. A speaker utter-
081
ance can admit multiple reasonable reactions [13, 23],
082
so agreement with one recorded response cannot fully

176
Existing datasets cover different aspects of dyadic in-
177

178

RealTalk [24] contains natural dyadic conver-

180

181

182

183

184
tener video. ResponseNet, introduced with OmniRe-
185
sponse [18], provides synchronized videos of both par-
186

187
behavior annotations. GLARE [11] adds visual reac-
188
tion categories and temporal intervals. These resources
189
already provide paired media and several types of be-
190
havior annotation. Their support for our task depends
191
on clip organization, role definitions, and coverage of
192
vocal feedback.
We further organize Seamless into
193
speaker-held-turn clips with aligned listener audiovi-
194
sual media, and use timestamped visual and vocal cues
195
to obtain ordered reaction descriptions.
196
Feedback evaluation has also moved beyond visual
197
quality. REACT2023 [22] and ReactFace [23] consider
198
multiple appropriate reactions. GLARE evaluates cat-
199
egory matches with temporal overlap. OmniResponse
200
and SpeakerVid-5M assess multimodal response qual-
201
ity and audiovisual synchronization, while OmniPer-
202
sona also evaluates the appropriateness of listening ex-
203

207

208
reference’s event times, but cannot by itself establish
209

210
dress short, intermittent feedback that may have mul-
211
