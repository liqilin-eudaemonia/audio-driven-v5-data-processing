Abstract
While talking head generation has advanced rapidly, generating natural listener
1
behavior in dyadic conversations, which know when to react, how to react, and
2
with what type of response, remains underexplored. Existing dyadic datasets
3
lack fine-grained listener reaction annotations, and prevailing evaluation metrics
4
inherited from talking-head and video generation measure visual realism rather than
5
whether a listener reacted appropriately. We address these gaps along three aspects.
6
First, we curate a listening-head-specific dataset built from RealTalk and Seamless
7
Interaction, comprising approximately 147 hours of paired speaker–listener videos
8
with 64,557 event-level reaction annotations across six categories: nodding, head
9
shaking, smiling, laughing, frowning, and surprised. Second, we introduce an
10
audio-driven baseline built on a flow-matching transformer, namely GLARE, with
11
prosody conditioning derived from Qwen2-Audio and a temporal reaction loss that
12
explicitly supervises frame-wise reactions. Third, we propose a reaction-oriented
13
evaluation protocol that jointly measures reaction occurrence (R-F1), temporal
14
alignment (R-tIoU), asymmetric temporal deviation (R-ATD), and reaction-region
15
visual quality (R-FID), giving a more behaviorally grounded assessment than
16
visual-quality-only metrics. Experiment results show consistent gains over prior
17
listening-head methods in both visual fidelity and reaction-level metrics, suggesting
18
that reaction-aware data, modeling, and evaluation are critical for natural listening
19
behavior.

Introduction
21
Recent advances in talking head generation have greatly improved lip synchronization, identity
22
preservation, and photorealistic rendering [14; 22; 32; 16; 18; 44; 12; 48]. Most existing methods,
23
however, focus on animating the speaker. In face-to-face conversation, the listener is equally important:
24
non-verbal behaviors, such as brief reactions like smiling and nodding, convey attention, agreement,
25
hesitation, and affective engagement [41]. The timing and type of these reactions strongly affect
26
perceived naturalness, since visually plausible motions can still appear unnatural when reactions are
27
absent, mistimed, or inconsistent with the conversational context [24; 25; 9].
28
Listening head generation has recently emerged as a distinct task. ViCo [47] introduced an early
29
benchmark, while subsequent works explored non-deterministic listener motion, language-aware
30
responses, emotion conditioning, improved sequence modeling, and real-time dyadic interaction [25;
31
26; 35; 20; 48; 15]. Despite this progress, reaction-aware listening head generation remains limited
32
by two key bottlenecks. First, existing datasets are not designed around fine-grained listener reactions.
33
ViCo provides task-specific supervision but is limited in scale, while larger dyadic resources such as
34
RealTalk [12], Seamless Interaction [1], and SpeakerVid-5M [45] offer rich conversational videos
35
without event-level annotations of reaction type, timing, and duration. As a result, current data
36
provide insufficient supervision for modeling when a listener should react, what reaction should
37
occur, and how the reaction should unfold over time.

Second, existing evaluation protocols mainly rely on metrics inherited from talking-head or video
39
generation, such as reconstruction fidelity, perceptual realism, and motion quality. These metrics are
40
useful for measuring global visual quality, but they do not explicitly assess whether generated listener
41
reactions are type-consistent, temporally aligned with conversational cues, or visually realistic within
42
reaction segments. This is particularly important because listener feedback is inherently multi-valid:
43
an appropriate response may not exactly match a single reference timestamp, yet its type, timing, and
44
quality should still be evaluated in a reaction-aware manner [9].
45
To address these limitations, we revisit listening head generation from the perspectives of data,
46
modeling, and evaluation. We curate a listening-head-specific dataset from RealTalk and Seamless
47
Interaction by extracting aligned speaker-listener portrait pairs and annotating listener reactions as
48
categorized temporal events. The resulting dataset contains approximately 147 hours of paired video
49
data and 64,557 reaction instances. Based on this dataset, we introduce an audio-driven listening
50
head generation method GLARE with prosody conditioning and a temporal reaction loss, enabling
51
the model to better capture speaker-side cues and generate appropriate listener reactions at the right
52
moment. We further propose reaction-centric evaluation metrics that measure reaction occurrence,
53
temporal alignment, asymmetric timing deviation, and reaction-region visual quality.
54
Our main contributions are as follows:
55
1. We curate a listening-head dataset built from dyadic conversational videos, with aligned video-
56
audio pairs and event-level annotations of listener reactions, comprising approximately 147 hours
57
of video data and 64,557 reaction instances.
58
2. We introduce an audio-driven reacting listener, namely GLARE, for listening head generation,
59
equipped with a dedicated prosody conditioning mechanism and a temporal reaction loss.
60
3. We propose reaction-oriented evaluation metrics for listening head generation that jointly measures
61
visual fidelity, reaction accuracy, and temporal alignment, enabling finer-grained assessment of
62
conversational appropriateness with extensive experiments comparing existing methods.

Related Works
64
Listening head generation.
Listening head generation synthesizes non-verbal listener behaviors
65
conditioned on speaker-side conversational cues. ViCo [46] first established responsive listening head
66
generation as a benchmark, and Learning2Listen [25] modeled listener motion as a non-deterministic
67
distribution to reflect the multi-valid nature of dyadic interaction. Later methods improved listener
68
synthesis with emotion conditioning [35], non-autoregressive sequence modeling [20], diffusion-
69
based generation [38], dyadic interaction modeling [42], and real-time multimodal interaction [48; 15].
70
However, most existing methods optimize holistic motion realism, diversity, or speaker-listener
71
synchrony, without explicitly modeling listener reactions as categorized and temporally localized
72
events. Our work instead focuses on generating reactions with appropriate type, timing and duration.
73
Interactive conversation datasets.
Dyadic conversation datasets provide important resources for
74
modeling social interaction. Early corpora such as RECOLA [33] and NoXi [5] support affective and
75
feedback behavior analysis, while ViCo [46] introduced a task-specific benchmark for responsive
76
listening heads. Larger resources such as RealTalk [12], Seamless [1], and SpeakerVid-5M [45]
77
provide more diverse conversational videos, but they are not designed around fine-grained listener
78
reaction events. In contrast, our dataset constructs aligned speaker-listener clips with explicit event-
79
level annotations of reaction type, timing, and duration.
80
Evaluation methods.
Listener generation is commonly evaluated using backchannel prediction
81
metrics, such as precision, recall, F1, tolerance windows, and subjective appropriateness judg-
82
ments [23; 10], or visual generation metrics such as FID [34], FVD [39], and LPIPS [43]. These
83
metrics measure feedback timing or global visual quality, but do not jointly assess whether a gener-
84
ated listener reacts with the correct type, at the correct moment, and with realistic reaction-specific
85
motion. Recent reaction generation benchmarks emphasize appropriateness, diversity, synchrony, and
86
realism [36; 37]. Our evaluation further treats reactions as categorized temporal events and measures
87
reaction occurrence, temporal alignment, asymmetric timing deviation, and reaction-region quality.

Dataset Curation
89
Our goal is to construct a listening-head-specific dataset that supports not only audio-driven listener
90
head generation, but also fine-grained modeling and evaluation of listener reactions. To this end,
91
we curate dyadic conversational videos into temporally aligned speaker–listener pairs and further
92
annotate the listener’s visible reactions as categorized temporal events. The overall pipeline is shown
93
in Fig. 1. Starting from RealTalk [11] and Seamless Interaction [1], we obtain 107,149 speaker
94
audio/listener-video pairs, corresponding to approximately 147 hours of data.
95
3.1
Speaker–Listener Clip Construction
96
As shown in Fig.1 (2) and (3), we first process the raw dyadic videos to obtain high-quality portrait
97
clips for both conversational participants. Since the original videos may contain scene transitions,
98
black frames, irrelevant content, multiple visible people, severe occlusions, or unstable face crops,
99
visual quality filtering is performed before clip extraction. For retained videos, each participant is
100
cropped into a portrait-centered video and an additional manual face-quality check is conducted to
101
remove clips with missing, partial, or poorly framed faces. This step ensures that the resulting listener
102
videos contain stable facial regions suitable for motion generation and reaction annotation.
103
We then parse the speaking and listening roles over time. For RealTalk, where the two speakers may
104
be mixed in the original audio, we perform audio separation, denoising, and speaker diarization to
105
estimate participant-level speech activity. For Seamless Interaction, we use the provided high-quality
106
audio streams and diarization results, with timestamp correction when necessary. Based on the
107
resulting speech-activity timelines, each dyadic conversation is decomposed into speaker-dominant
108
intervals. For each interval, we pair the active speaker’s audio with the temporally synchronized
109
portrait video of the other participant, who is treated as the listener. This produces paired training
110
examples in the form of speaker audio and listener video, while preserving the conversational timing
111
between the two participants.
112
Implementation details of speaker-listener clip reconstruction, including filtering criteria, face-crop
113
procedure, diarization correction, and quality-control procedures, are provided in Appendix A.

3.2
Reaction Taxonomy and Annotation
115
A central objective of our dataset is to explicitly capture listener reactions that are semantically
116
meaningful and temporally localized. We focus on six common reaction categories: nodding, head
117
shaking, smiling, laughing, frowning, and surprised, which are visually recognizable and frequently
118
associated with conversational feedback and affective responses [25; 47; 12]. Compared with micro-
119
behaviors (e.g., eye blinking or gaze shifts), and background motions (e.g., subtle pose adjustments),
120
these reactions have four desirable properties: they convey clear communicative or affective intent,
121
are visually recognizable and are strongly correlated with the conversational context. These properties
122
make them suitable targets for reaction-aware listener generation and reaction-centric evaluation.
123
As shown in Fig.1 (4), to annotate listener reactions at scale, we design a visual reaction detector to
124
the cropped listener videos. For a listener clip with T frames, the detector outputs a dense reaction
125
score vector r ∈[0, 1]T ×6, where each channel corresponds to one of the six reaction categories.
126
The detector combines head-motion cues, facial landmark dynamics, facial expression cues, and
127
temporal smoothing to estimate frame-wise reaction confidence. Continuous high-confidence regions
128
are then converted into event-level annotations A = {(yi, ri, ts,i, te,i)}N
i=1, where yi ∈Y is the event
129
type from the six reaction classes, ri is the frame-wise reaction score within [ts,i, te,i], and ts,i and
130
te,i are the start and end times. Each final event is assigned a single type, and cross-class overlaps
131
are resolved by keeping the dominant reaction. The resulting annotations therefore provide both
132
sparse temporal boundaries for event-level evaluation and dense frame-wise scores for reaction-aware
133
training supervision.
134
We further conduct manual verification to remove unreliable detections and improve annotation
135
quality. The final curated dataset contains 64,557 reaction instances in total. The distribution across
136
reaction categories is summarized in Table 1. Smiling and head shaking are the most frequent reaction
137
types, while surprised reactions occur less often, reflecting their relatively sparse and context-specific
138
nature in natural conversations. The detailed detector design, including class-specific visual cues,
139
smoothing strategies, event merging rules, and thresholds, can be referred to Appendix B.

6.1
Experiment Settings
245
Implementation details. We implement our listening-head generator with a latent flow-matching
246
transformer and train it with mixed precision using Accelerate on 4×L40s GPUs. We use AdamW
247
(lr=5e-4), gradient clipping (1.0), cosine decay with warmup, batch size 256, and train for 650 epochs.
248
The default temporal setup is 25 FPS, and audio is sampled at 16 kHz. Input frames are resized to
249
512×512 and normalized to [−1, 1]. We split the train/test set into a ratio 9:1. The pretrained motion
250
autoencoder [40] and audio LLM [8] are kept frozen. More details can be found in Appendix C.1.
251
Evaluation metrics. We use Fréchet Inecption Distance (FID) [34] and 16-frame Fréchet Video
252
Distance (FVD) [39] to assess image and video generation quality; Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio
253
(PSNR) for measuring pixel fidelity; Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) for perceived change in
254
structural information; Variation (Var) [25] for motion diversity and richness; Learned Perceptual
255
Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [43] for measuring perceptual distance; Residual Pearson Correlation
256
Coefficient (rPCC) to measure the correlation between motions of speaker and listener; DI-Sync [27]
257
to quantify the causal coordination between the speaker’s verbal cues and the listener’s non-verbal
258
responses. We also use metrics proposed in Sec. 5 (i.e., R-F1, R-tIoU, R-ATD, and R-FID) to
259
specifically evaluate the accuracy and generation quality of reactions.
260
Comparison methods. We choose L2L [25], DIM [38], ViCo [47], ListenFormer [20], and DyStream
261
[6] for comparison. All methods are trained and tested using our dataset, respectively.

Ablation Study
287
We conduct our ablation studies on RealTalk dataset. More ablation studies, including speaker audio
288

289
Module effectiveness. We ablate the effectiveness of the prosody conditioning mechanism and
290
temporal reaction loss in Table 4a. It can be seen that, adding the prosody condition improves
291
visual quality and motion dynamics (e.g., PSNR, FVD, Var), but brings limited gains on reaction-
292
related metrics and even degrades temporal precision (R-ATD), indicating that prosodic cues alone
293
are insufficient for accurate reaction modeling. In contrast, the reaction loss yields substantial
294
improvements across various metrics (DI-Sync, R-F1, R-tIoU) and significantly reduces R-ATD and
295
R-FID, demonstrating its key role in learning temporally aligned and semantically correct reactions.
296
Combining both modules achieves the best overall performance, showing that prosody provides
297
complementary temporal cues on top of explicit reaction supervision.
298
Reaction loss coefficient λreact. Table 4b shows that increasing the reaction loss coefficient λreact
299
from a small value (0.01) to a moderate range (0.05–0.1) consistently improves both visual quality
300
and reaction-related metrics, indicating that stronger supervision helps the model learn more accurate
301
and temporally aligned behaviors. However, further increasing λreact (e.g., ≥0.2) leads to degraded
302
performance in both generation quality and reaction accuracy, suggesting that overly strong supervi-
303
sion disrupts the balance between the two objectives. Overall, λreact=0.05 achieves the best trade-off
304
across metrics.

A.2
Video Filtering and Face-Crop Quality Control
467
The raw videos contain several types of content that are unsuitable for listening head generation,
468
including abrupt scene transitions, blackout frames, irrelevant scenes, severe occlusion, unstable
469
viewpoints, and cases where more than two people appear in the visible region. To remove these
470
samples efficiently, we uniformly sample frames from each video at 2.5-second intervals and perform
471
a thumbnail-based inspection. Videos with frequent scene changes, missing faces, severe visual
472
artifacts, or excessive participant overlap are discarded. We then conduct a second verification pass
473
on the retained videos to further remove visually unstable or semantically irrelevant clips.
474
For each retained dyadic video, we separately crop the two participants into portrait-centered videos.
475
We use Face-Alignment [4] to detect the facial region in the first frame where a valid face is observed,
476
enlarge the detected bounding box by a scale factor of 1.5, and apply the resulting crop to the
477
full video. The enlargement preserves contextual head motion and avoids overly tight crops that
478
would remove meaningful listener reactions. After cropping, we re-run face detection and landmark
479
localization on the cropped videos to identify low-quality samples. A cropped video is discarded if it
480
contains missing face detections, partial faces, abnormal facial aspect ratios, unstable face proportions,
481
or strong non-frontal views that prevent reliable motion extraction and reaction annotation.
482
A.3
Audio Processing and Speaker–Listener Pairing
483
For RealTalk, the raw audio contain mixed speech from both participants. We first apply the Asteroid
484
toolkit [28] for source separation and then perform audio denoising to improve the quality of each
485
separated stream. The separated streams are manually checked to remove severe separation failures.
486
We then use PyAnnote [31; 3] to estimate speech-activity boundaries for each participant. These
487
boundaries define the time intervals in which one participant is treated as the active speaker and the
488
other participant is treated as the listener.
489
For Seamless Interaction, the audio streams are generally cleaner and often already separated, with
490
small portion of them including noises. We use the provided audio and diarization information
491
when reliable, and apply PyAnnote-based timestamp correction when the provided boundaries are
492
inaccurate. After obtaining participant-level speech-activity timelines, we decompose each dyadic
493
conversation into a sequence of speaker-dominant intervals. For each interval, we pair the active
494
speaker’s audio with the temporally synchronized cropped video of the other participant (listener).
495
This produces paired samples of the form (aspeaker, V listener), where aspeaker denotes the speaker audio
496
and V listener denotes the listener video.

562

B.4
Manual Verification
576

577
reliability. During verification, we remove events caused by tracking failure, scene change, face
578
occlusion, unstable crops, non-listening behavior, or expression ambiguity. We also discard events
579
whose visual evidence is too weak to support the assigned reaction label. This verification step is
580
applied after the detector has produced candidate intervals, so the final annotations retain the temporal

More Ablation Studies
702
Due to space limit in the main paper, here we present three more ablation studies, including input
703
speaker audio length, prosody channel projection, and temporal reaction loss type.
704
Input speaker audio length. Different length of input audio from speaker can provide different
705
amount of contexts. Table 9 shows that increasing speaker’s audio length generally improves
706
performance, as longer temporal context provides richer cues for both motion generation and reaction
707
prediction. Performance improves notably from 2s to 10s across most metrics, including visual quality
708
(PSNR, FVD) and reaction accuracy (DI-Sync, R-F1, R-tIoU). However, further increasing the length
709
(e.g., 20s or 50s) brings marginal gains or even slight degradation, likely due to increased temporal
710
redundancy and modeling difficulty. Overall, 10s achieves the best balance between sufficient context
711
and stable modeling.
712
Prosody Channel Projection. In our architecture, we project the channel dimension from 1 of frame-
713
aligned prosodic intensity scalar p into 4 of prosody condition w˜p. We ablate this channel projection
714
in Table 10. The results show that introducing a learnable channel projection for the prosodic
715
intensity signal consistently improves performance compared to using the raw scalar (channel=1),
716
indicating the benefit of increasing representation capacity for prosody conditioning. As the channel
717
dimension increases from 1 to 4, we observe steady gains in both generation quality (e.g., lower FVD,
718
LPIPS, and rPCC) and reaction-related metrics (e.g., higher R-tIoU and lower R-ATD), suggesting
719
improved temporal alignment and more accurate reaction dynamics. However, further increasing the
720
channel dimension to 8 does not bring consistent improvements and slightly degrades several metrics,
721
implying diminishing returns and potential over-parameterization. Overall, projecting the prosodic
722
signal to a moderate channel size (4) achieves the best trade-off between expressiveness and stability,
723
and is thus adopted in our final model.

F
Discussion of Limitations and Future Works
732
Despite the improvements achieved in reaction-aware listening head generation, several limitations
733
remain. First, listener reactions are inherently multi-valid and subjective: given the same conversa-
734
tional cue, different listeners may exhibit different reaction types, timings, or intensities. Our current
735
formulation evaluates generated reactions against a single reference, which cannot fully capture this
736
diversity. Second, our work focuses on a set of explicit actual reactions (e.g., nodding, smiling),
737
while listener behavior, more broadly, also includes micro-behaviors (e.g., subtle gaze or facial
738
changes) and baseline dynamics (e.g., natural head motion and posture drift showing the person is
739
not stationary). These components are more continuous and harder to annotate, and are not explicitly
740
modeled in our current framework.
741
In addition, listener behavior is influenced by factors beyond audio prosody, such as language
742
semantics, personality, and conversational context, which are only partially captured in our current
743
model. Extending toward richer multimodal understanding and more comprehensive modeling of
744
listener motion remains an important direction for future work.
745
G
Broader Impact
746
This work aims to improve the naturalness and appropriateness of listener behavior in conversational
747
agents, with potential applications in virtual assistants, social robots, telepresence, and human-
748
computer interaction systems. By enabling more responsive and context-aware listener reactions, our
749
approach may contribute to more engaging and human-like interactions.
750
At the same time, improved generation of realistic listener behaviors may introduce risks related to
751
synthetic media, such as creating misleading or deceptive conversational content. In addition, listener
752
reactions can implicitly convey agreement or emotional alignment, which may be misinterpreted
753
or misused in certain contexts. We emphasize that this work is intended for research purposes,
754
and responsible deployment should include appropriate safeguards such as clear disclosure of syn-
755
thetic content, adherence to dataset licensing terms, and consideration of fairness and diversity in
756
conversational behaviors.
